\section{Environment setup and basic policy gradient}\label{sec:setup}
The implementations use PyTorch and REINFORCE-style Monte Carlo policy gradients~\cite{williams}. Gymnasium is used as the maintained successor to Gym. The exact environment IDs are \texttt{CartPole-v1} and \texttt{ALE/Pong-v5}; the latter is the official namespaced Pong-v5 environment~\cite{cartdocs,pongdocs}. No v0 or v4 substitute is used.

\subsection{What the symbols mean}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}p{0.15\linewidth}X@{}}
\toprule
Symbol & Meaning\\
\midrule
$t$, $T$ & Decision index within a complete episode, and that episode's length.\\
$s_t$ & Policy input before decision $t$: four physical measurements for CartPole, or image-derived features for Pong. It denotes an observation, not a claim of full observability.\\
$a_t$, $r_t$ & Action selected at $t$ and reward received immediately afterward.\\
$\theta$, $\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$ & Policy-network parameters and the probability of action $a$ at input $s$.\\
$\gamma$, $G_t$ & Discount factor and discounted reward remaining from decision $t$.\\
$b_t$ & Baseline subtracted from $G_t$; zero for Part 1.\\
$B$, $i$ & Number of complete episodes in an update, and the index of one episode.\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}

The objective is the expected discounted reward of a complete episode:
\begin{equation}
J(\theta)=\E_{\pi_\theta}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^t r_t\right],
\qquad G_t=\sum_{k=t}^{T-1}\gamma^{k-t}r_k.
\end{equation}
The expectation averages environment and action randomness. $G_t$ is computed backward using $G_t=r_t+\gamma G_{t+1}$, with zero continuation after the episode. The plots instead show \textbf{undiscounted episode reward}, $\sum_t r_t$, as required by the assignment.

For each complete batch, Adam minimizes
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L=-\frac{c}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}\sum_{t=0}^{T_i-1}
\gamma^t\log\pi_\theta(a_t^{(i)}\mid s_t^{(i)})\bigl(G_t^{(i)}-b_t^{(i)}\bigr).
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
Here $c$ is a fixed positive scale: 0.05 for CartPole and 1 for Pong. Scaling changes gradient magnitude but not the objective's optimum. The outer $\gamma^t$ is retained for the stated discounted episodic objective. In Part 1, $b_t=0$: returns are not centered or standardized, and no implicit baseline is introduced.



A logit is an unnormalized action score; softmax converts the two scores into probabilities. Actions are sampled, including during evaluation. Gradients accumulate over the complete batch before an update; Pong computes them in memory-saving chunks without stepping between chunks.

\section{Baselines, task details, and evaluation protocol}\label{sec:baseline}
\subsection{CartPole: learned state-and-time baseline}
CartPole observes cart position, cart velocity, pole angle and pole angular velocity. Actions push left or right. Default rewards are $+1$ per transition, including failure; an episode ends on physical failure or truncation at 500 decisions~\cite{cartdocs}. Both flags finish the return calculation, and accumulation never crosses an episode boundary.

The Part 2 baseline $b_\psi(s_t,t)$ is a separate network: five inputs (the four observations plus $t/500$), 64 tanh units and one output predicting $G_t$. $\psi$ denotes its weights. Predictions use weights from earlier batches and are detached from the actor loss. After the actor update, five Adam steps at learning rate 0.01 minimize mean squared error between predictions and full Monte Carlo targets over the batch's observed decisions. There is no temporal-difference bootstrapping.

\subsection{Pong: visual input and constant baseline}
Pong retains v5 defaults: frame skip 4, sticky-action probability 0.25, and an emulator limit of 108,000 frames. Discounting is per agent decision, not per raw emulator frame. The policy samples \textbf{only ALE actions 2 and 3} (RIGHT and LEFT), as assigned; no FIRE or NOOP startup action is added.

The RGB image is cropped to rows 35--194, downsampled by two, and its red channel is binarized after removing background colors 144 and 109, matching the provided preprocessing. The policy receives the flattened difference between consecutive $80\times80$ binary frames, as float32 at inference. The input is zero on reset. This supplies motion information, but is not guaranteed to be a sufficient Markov state. Full games, not scoring rallies, are episodes; reward-to-go is \emph{not} reset after a point.

For Part 2, the baseline is a scalar constant within a batch. Starting at $b_0=0$, update it only after using the current batch:
\begin{equation}
\bar G_k=\frac{\sum_{i,t}\gamma^t G_t^{(i)}}{\sum_{i,t}\gamma^t},
\qquad b_{k+1}=0.95b_k+0.05\bar G_k.
\end{equation}
The index $k$ counts updates, not game decisions. Thus the current batch uses only prior data to set its baseline.

An action-independent baseline does not change the expected \emph{unclipped} policy gradient: conditional on the current input and history,
\begin{equation}
\E_a[b\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)]
=b\nabla_\theta\sum_a\pi_\theta(a\mid s)=0.
\end{equation}
Here $\nabla_\theta$ is the vector of derivatives with respect to policy parameters, and $\E_a$ averages over sampled actions. Subtracting a predictable return can remove variation unrelated to which action was chosen; it does not change game rewards.

\subsection{Checkpoint and evaluation choices}
The main results use \textbf{final checkpoints after fixed budgets}; every model receives 500 fresh stochastic evaluation episodes, with no learning. Reset seeds are separate from training and shared across methods. CartPole's primary seed 1668 was fixed before full experiments; all three seeds are retained. Pong also saved validation-selected checkpoints using 32 reserved games every 1,024 training games; their separate test results are supplementary, not substituted for final-checkpoint results.

Pong uses the permitted alternative learning rate 0.001. On identical real-game data, one update at 0.01 changed the policy more (mean KL divergence $3.423\times10^{-4}$) than 0.001 ($1.996\times10^{-6}$). KL divergence measures change in the action distribution. This supports a conservative step-size choice, \emph{not} a claim that 0.01 was proven unstable. All CartPole gradients stayed below their cap. Pong clipped 1,542/2,500 basic-method updates (61.68\%) and 466/2,500 baseline updates (18.64\%). Clipping is an additional stabilizing modification; the formal unbiasedness argument does not extend to the clipped estimator.

\subsection{Resource and software record}
CartPole used CPU only. Pong used one RTX 5060 Ti (GPU 0), with both actors in one GPU process and eight CPU emulator workers. It completed both 20,000-game training runs and all test evaluations in approximately 2.64 hours, below the 16-hour training ceiling. Only one training seed was used for Pong; there are no unreported Pong training-seed replicates.

Both environments used Python 3.12, PyTorch 2.14.0+cu130, NumPy 2.5.3 and Gymnasium 1.2.2; Pong additionally used ALE 0.11.2. The CUDA-enabled PyTorch installation was reused for CartPole, but CartPole models and tensors stayed on CPU. Exact configurations and source hashes are saved with the results.

\section{Correctness and edge-case experiments: CartPole}\label{sec:carttests}
The following experiments test numerical predictions and task behavior. They complement the observed improvement over random policies; they do not use AI's agreement as evidence of correctness. Run \texttt{python validate.py} in the CartPole project to execute them.

\subsection{Return calculation and gradient direction}
For rewards $(1,1,1)$ and $\gamma=0.95$, the independently calculated returns are $(2.8525,1.95,1)$. The implementation agrees to float32 precision. Setting $\gamma=0$ returns the immediate rewards exactly. These experiments check discount exponents and the absence of reward leakage between decisions.

In an exactly enumerable two-step problem, both actions follow a Bernoulli probability $p=\operatorname{sigmoid}(\theta)$, and each reward equals the binary action. The exact objective is $(1+\gamma)p$; its derivative is $(1+\gamma)p(1-p)$. Summing the policy-gradient estimator over all four action sequences produced 0.471188756, with or without a constant baseline, compared with analytic derivative 0.471188748. This checks the reward-to-go and outer-discount convention as well as baseline invariance.

A positive-return update for one selected action increased its probability from 0.500000 to 0.502500. An incorrect loss sign would reverse this direction.

\subsection{Physical termination, truncation and reproducibility}
Two runs with a fixed reset seed and the same ten actions produced identical state sequences. Separately placing the cart beyond its allowed position and the pole beyond its allowed angle caused physical termination; the terminal step still received $+1$. A one-decision time-limit experiment caused truncation without physical failure. The implementation ends on either flag, resets before another episode, and uses zero continuation after the specified episodic horizon. This checks end-condition logic against known environment behavior, rather than only checking array formats.

The full ten-decision trace is inspected in Section~\ref{sec:trace}. It records state, action probabilities, sampled actions and rewards so individual decisions can be examined independently of a learning curve.

\subsection{Does the learned baseline reduce gradient variance?}
A policy was frozen at initialization. A separate baseline was fitted on 512 episodes using 150 MSE/Adam steps, then both gradient estimators were evaluated on the \emph{same} 512 different trajectories. This is a separate variance experiment, not the evolving five-critic-steps-per-training-batch procedure. Summing sample variances over policy parameters gave:

\section{Correctness and edge-case experiments: Pong}\label{sec:pongtests}
Pong was launched only after 15 preflight checks passed. Preflight evidence is preserved in \path{preflight/results.json}. The checks include the following task-specific predictions and controls.

\subsection{Returns across points and action restrictions}
For rewards $(0,1,0,-1)$ and $\gamma=0.99$, the expected reward-to-go values are
\[ (G_0,G_1,G_2,G_3)=(0.019701,0.0199,-0.99,-1). \]
The implementation matched these within $10^{-6}$. In particular, the negative reward still affects returns before the earlier positive point: returns are not accidentally reset at scoring boundaries. A separate zero-discount test returned the immediate rewards.

The two-step enumerated-gradient experiment was repeated at $\gamma=0.99$ and agreed with its analytic derivative within $10^{-7}$, with and without an action-independent baseline. A positive-return update increased the chosen action's probability; zero returns without a baseline produced zero gradient.

Complete preflight games generated scoring events and reached an episode end using only actions 2 and 3. Runtime workers reject any other agent action, and verify the environment's action names. This tests actual progress without silently introducing a FIRE or NOOP wrapper. Recorded final evaluations contained 500 normal terminations and zero truncations for each model, ruling out evaluation scores generated by an accidentally shortened game.

\subsection{Visual preprocessing and stochastic replay}
Synthetic images with known pixels checked the crop, factor-two sampling, removal of colors 144 and 109, preservation of foreground pixels, and lack of mutation of the original RGB frame. Real-game inputs were checked to be zero on reset and otherwise to take values only in $\{-1,0,1\}$ after differencing. Resetting the previous frame prevents an artificial motion signal from leaking between games.

Two emulator runs with the same reset seed and fixed actions produced identical frame/reward hashes even with v5's sticky actions. This demonstrates controlled reproducibility without removing the stochasticity required by the chosen environment version. Stochastic policy actions remain separate from emulator randomness.

\subsection{Learning-rate, resource and recovery experiments}
Two copies of the same initial policy received one update using identical full-game data. Mean forward KL from the old policy was $3.423\times10^{-4}$ at learning rate 0.01 and $1.996\times10^{-6}$ at 0.001. These quantify relative update size, not a long-run learning-rate comparison.

Preflight checked exactly one visible CUDA device and performed real GPU updates. Both policies used that one device; eight CPU workers ran the emulators. The complete suite finished in approximately 2.64 hours. There was no multi-GPU training.

An actor and its Adam state were saved after an update, restored into another copy, and both received the same next fixed-data update. The resulting parameter hashes matched exactly. This tests optimizer recovery, not an extra on-policy learning experiment. Source/config hashes, finite parameters, backup checkpoints, independent episode RNG seeds and saved evaluation progress protected the long run. Initial actors matched across methods, and every current batch used a baseline frozen from earlier data. These operational checks supplement the gradient and behavior experiments; they do not establish successful task learning.

\section{Pong baseline variance over training}\label{sec:pongvariance}
A baseline is intended to reduce variance in gradient estimates, not necessarily the variance of episode rewards. To isolate its effect, each probe freezes one actor and computes both estimators on the same held-out trajectories, without updating that actor or its training statistics.

At initialization, a candidate constant baseline was estimated from eight independent games and measured on 16 held-out games. The summed per-parameter gradient variance changed from 369.320 to 20.763, a \PongInitialVarianceReduction\% reduction. This is a \emph{separately fitted candidate}, not the actual first training batch's EMA baseline, which starts at zero.

For actors trained without a baseline, a hypothetical constant was fitted on eight separate games solely for the probe; it was never used in those actors' training. For actors trained with the EMA baseline, the probe used the actual frozen EMA value from prior training. These are within-actor comparisons: comparing variance levels \emph{between different actors} would confound the baseline with changes in policy.

The actual EMA baseline's ratios were 0.873, 1.017, 0.924 and 1.011. It therefore reduced the measured variance at some checkpoints and slightly increased it at others. The separately fitted baseline also stopped consistently helping later basic-method actors. These findings do not support a claim of sustained variance reduction from this constant baseline. With only 16 trajectories per checkpoint, the estimates are noisy, and no confidence interval for the ratios was computed.

Each probe also saves the difference between empirical mean gradients and its paired standard-error measure, plus a check that actor parameters remain unchanged. The analytic expected-gradient identity is stronger than finite-sample equality. In addition, these variance probes measure the \emph{unclipped} estimator; the frequent clipping during actual Pong training is a separate source of bias and altered update variability.

These results motivate a state-dependent baseline and more samples per variance probe as future ablations. They do not justify changing or selectively omitting the reported constant-baseline run.

\section{How to reproduce and validate the results}\label{sec:reproduce}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
The repository is organized into \path{mlds_assignment1_cartpole}, \path{mlds_assignment1_pong}, and \path{mlds_assignment1_policy_gradient_report}. All algorithm, validation, plotting and report-generation source is included. Preserve the recorded outputs before rerunning commands that write into their run directories.

\subsection{CartPole: train, evaluate and run validation}
In a Python environment with PyTorch, install the CartPole requirements and run:
\begin{verbatim}
cd mlds_assignment1_cartpole
python -m pip install -r requirements.txt
python cartpole.py suite --episodes 8000 --lr 0.01
python validate.py
jupyter lab cartpole_plots.ipynb
\end{verbatim}
The suite trains all six models and evaluates each for 500 episodes. The validation script executes the experiments in Section~\ref{sec:carttests}, raises an error on a failed assertion, and writes \path{validation/validation.json}. The notebook regenerates plots without training. To evaluate one saved model:
\begin{verbatim}
python cartpole.py evaluate \
  --checkpoint runs/no_baseline_seed1668/last.pt
\end{verbatim}

\subsection{Pong: preflight and a fresh single-GPU reproduction}
Activate \path{~/mlds_assignment1_pong_env} on deepdish5. On a new host, first create that virtual environment and install the Pong \path{requirements.txt}. For a fresh reproduction, copy \path{pong_env.py}, \path{pong_core.py}, \path{run_pong.py}, \path{plot_results.py}, \path{supervise.sh} and \path{config.json} into a new directory, without old checkpoints or preflight outputs. Then run:
\begin{verbatim}
export CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES=GPU-cef65388-88b0-f39a-6bac-c5316eb78540
export CUBLAS_WORKSPACE_CONFIG=:4096:8

nohup setsid bash supervise.sh > nohup.log 2>&1 < /dev/null &

\section{What would be tested with three additional months?}\label{sec:future}
\subsection{CartPole: distinguish reliable learning from a favorable seed}
Run substantially more seeds with separate hyperparameter-validation and final-test resets. Sweep Adam learning rate and batch size under matched episode budgets. Compare the current learned state/time baseline with a constant baseline and different critic capacities or fitting frequencies. Measure critic error and paired gradient variance at early, middle and late actor checkpoints, not only at initialization. This would test whether baseline approximation or update lag contributes to the weaker primary run.

Stress-test the final policies using controlled initial pole angles, cart velocities and changed pole/cart physics. Evaluate stochastic and greedy policies separately. These experiments would determine whether the perfect primary evaluation generalizes beyond the standard small reset region and whether the observed cross-seed variability persists.

\subsection{Pong: investigate failure with controlled ablations}
First, retain the required v5 environment and actions 2/3, but compare frame differencing with two- or four-frame stacks and a small convolutional policy. This tests whether the present features discard useful position information. Track point outcomes, first-point performance and complete-game score separately, without replacing full games with point episodes in the main comparison.

Second, examine credit assignment: measure how much of the unclipped gradient comes from successive time windows, and compare discounting conventions only in explicitly labeled sensitivity experiments. Keep the required $\gamma=0.99$ result as the main experiment. For example, restarting returns at points would change the objective and must not be presented as the original full-game algorithm.

Third, tune learning rate, gradient cap and batch size using held-out validation seeds. Compare clipping frequencies and action saturation as well as returns. Repeat with several training seeds, and reserve untouched seeds for final 500-game evaluations. Longer training alone would not distinguish a poor representation, optimization problems and a weak late-game reward signal.

\subsection{Baselines: test the mechanism, not only the score}
For Pong, compare the current lagged scalar with learned state-dependent and time-dependent baselines. Fit and test each on independent trajectories at the same frozen actors. Use hundreds of paired trajectories per probe, bootstrap uncertainty for variance ratios, and compare clipped and unclipped gradients. This would establish when a baseline helps, whether apparent small increases are sampling noise, and whether reduced variance translates into better task performance.
